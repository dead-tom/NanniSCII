# Artist statement

For this project, I drew on the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's Consumer Complaint Database, an archive of millions of grievances from real people against banks, lenders and credit providers, each one a modern descendant of Nanni's tablet. Only narratives shared with the consent of their authors were ever published, and every personal detail has been scrubbed from the record, reduced to a string of anonymous "XXXX", so that what remains is not the person but the pattern of their frustration. In September 2026, weeks before this project began, the Bureau withdrew the narratives from its public database; they now survive only in its FOIA archive. Selecting a single line from each complaint, I used a machine-translation model (praeclarum/cuneiform, run through CuneiScribe, an experimental cuneiform library) to translate the English into Akkadian and convert it into Unicode cuneiform signs. Those signs then became the alphabet of an image, drawn once more as ASCII art, the alphabet through which machines first learned to read us. The translations are approximations made by a machine, not a scholar: Akkadian has no word for an overdraft fee or a credit score, and so the ancient language carries the exasperation while the modern details fall away. In this sense the work enacts the argument of the essay itself. These are human voices passed through the archive, read and rewritten by an artificial intelligence, and returned to us in a form that is recognisably ours, and yet no longer quite our own.
